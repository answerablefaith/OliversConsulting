Automation readiness checklist

Automation readiness checklist for small businesses

Before you automate a process, check whether the process is ready. Clear ownership, rules, data, exceptions and success measures matter more than the tool you choose.

Published 14 September 2026Updated 14 September 2026By Henry OliverAutomation strategy and decision content
Quick answer

A small-business process is ready for automation when seven things are sufficiently clear: who owns the outcome, what triggers the work, what the normal route is, which data is required, what counts as an exception, which controls must remain, and how success will be measured. If several of those are still disputed, automation is likely to make the process faster without making it better.

Choosing a good process to automate is only the first decision. The next question is whether that process is actually ready to be built.

A process can look repetitive from the outside and still be a poor automation candidate because the team is quietly making judgement calls, fixing missing data or interpreting different rules each time. Those hidden decisions usually surface during testing.

My view: automation readiness is not about having perfect documentation. It is about knowing enough about the real process that the workflow can make a safe decision, stop when it is uncertain and hand the right exception to a person.

The seven-point automation readiness checklist

Use this checklist on one specific workflow. Do not assess “sales”, “finance” or “operations” as a whole.

Readiness areaReady enough to testWarning sign
1. OwnerOne person or role owns the result and can resolve exceptionsResponsibility changes depending on who notices the problem
2. TriggerThe workflow has a clear start eventWork begins when somebody remembers to check
3. Normal rulesThe standard route can be explained consistentlyDifferent people describe different “normal” processes
4. DataRequired fields and trusted sources are identifiableImportant inputs are missing, duplicated or spread across unknown sources
5. ExceptionsCommon exceptions are known and can be routed for reviewMost cases are treated as special cases
6. ControlsApprovals, validations and stop rules are explicitThe current process relies on informal judgement nobody can describe
7. Success measureThe team can define what should improve after automationSuccess is simply “we automated it”
Simple readiness score

Score each area 0, 1 or 2: 0 = unclear, 1 = partly defined, 2 = clear enough to test. A score of 11–14 suggests the process is usually ready for a controlled pilot. A lower score does not mean “never automate”; it tells you where process design work is needed first. This is an Olivers Consulting decision aid, not an industry benchmark.

1. Is there a clear process owner?

Automation needs an owner for the outcome, not only a technical owner for the workflow.

If an invoice fails validation, who decides what happens next? If two CRM records match, who chooses the correct one? If a report total looks wrong, who owns the source data?

Without an owner, the exception queue becomes a new inbox that everybody assumes somebody else is checking.

2. What exactly triggers the workflow?

A strong automation begins with an observable event: a form is submitted, a status changes, a file arrives, an invoice becomes overdue, an order is approved or a scheduled reporting period closes.

“When the team thinks it is time” is not a trigger. If the current process begins because somebody remembers to look in a folder or inbox, define that start condition before building.

3. Can the normal route be written down?

You do not need a forty-page procedure. You do need agreement on what should happen when nothing unusual occurs.

Write the normal path in simple steps: trigger, required inputs, checks, action, destination and completion state. If the team immediately responds with “it depends”, write down what it depends on.

If every branch is a judgement call, automate the preparation around the decision rather than the decision itself.

4. Is the data good enough for this workflow?

You do not need perfect enterprise data. You need the fields required for this specific decision to be identifiable and testable.

For example, cross-system customer updates may require a stable customer ID, company name, billing contact and destination record. If the workflow cannot identify the customer confidently, the correct action may be to stop for review rather than create another record.

5. Do you know the exceptions?

Automation is easiest to trust when the normal route and the exception route are both designed.

List the common cases that should stop, escalate or behave differently. Missing mandatory fields, duplicate matches, unusual values, approval thresholds and customer-specific arrangements are all examples.

You do not need to predict every future edge case. You do need a safe default: if the workflow cannot apply the normal rule confidently, it should stop visibly rather than improvise.

I would rather launch a workflow that automates 80% of cases cleanly and routes 20% for review than force 100% straight-through processing by hiding uncertainty.

6. Which controls must remain after automation?

Some checks exist because the process is manual. Others exist because the decision matters.

Keep material controls such as approval thresholds, reconciliations, record matching, mandatory-field validation and audit evidence where they still serve a purpose. Remove duplicate checking only when the new workflow makes it redundant.

This is where automation and process redesign meet. A control should not survive merely because “we have always done it”, but it should not disappear merely because software can complete the next step automatically.

7. How will you know the automation worked?

Define one or two measures before the build. Useful measures include handling time, backlog, error rework, response delay, number of manual touches, exception rate or time to complete the process.

Do not require a perfect ROI model for a small pilot. But do establish a baseline. If you cannot describe what should improve, it will be difficult to tell whether the new workflow created value or merely moved work somewhere else.

What if the process fails the checklist?

Do not abandon automation automatically. Use the failed areas as a short improvement backlog.

  • No owner? Assign one before building.
  • No clear trigger? Standardise the intake route.
  • Rules disputed? Agree the normal path and stop conditions.
  • Data unreliable? Define the minimum required fields and trusted source.
  • Exceptions dominate? Narrow the scope to the stable part.
  • Controls unclear? Separate genuine risk controls from historical habits.
  • No success measure? Record a simple baseline for a few weeks.

Sometimes this process-design work creates value even before automation starts because the team finally agrees how the work is supposed to operate.

When is a process ready for a pilot?

It is ready when the team can show the trigger, walk through the normal path, identify the data, name the common exceptions, explain the important controls and agree how the result will be measured.

The pilot does not need to automate everything. Start with the dependable route and make exceptions visible. That gives the business real evidence about data quality, exception volume and support needs before expanding scope.

Common questions

How do you know if a process is ready for automation?

Check whether the owner, trigger, normal rules, required data, exceptions, controls and success measures are clear enough to test. If several are still disputed, fix those areas first.

Do you need perfect data before automating?

No. You need data that is good enough for the workflow, with validation and a safe route for missing, duplicate or ambiguous inputs.

What should stop an automation project from starting?

Pause when nobody owns the outcome, rules are unclear, exceptions dominate, important data cannot be identified reliably or the team cannot say how success will be measured.

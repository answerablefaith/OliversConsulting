Reporting automation

The Monday report your ecommerce team should not be building by hand

A weekly report should help the business decide what to do next. If Monday morning is spent exporting data, repairing spreadsheets and chasing numbers, the report is arriving too late.

Published 7 July 2026Updated 26 July 2026By Henry OliverUK ecommerce & wholesale
Quick answer

Your Monday report should be generated from trusted sources before the week starts. Sales, margin, stock cover, channel performance and exceptions should arrive ready to review. Automate the collection, checks and formatting. Keep the commercial judgement with the operator.

There is a particular kind of report that looks harmless because everyone is used to it. Someone exports Shopify sales, downloads marketplace data, checks stock, copies numbers into a spreadsheet, repairs a formula, adds notes and sends it around the business.

By the time the report is ready, Monday is half gone. Worse, the person building it has spent their best operating hours preparing information instead of acting on it.

I learned this running a multi-channel ecommerce operation with around 200,000 SKUs across Shopify, Amazon and eBay. At that scale, reporting cannot depend on memory, copy and paste or one spreadsheet only one person understands.

My view is that a weekly report is not a document. It is a decision system. If it does not shorten the distance between a problem appearing and someone acting, it is just formatted delay.

Separate preparation from judgement

A good weekly report has two parts: mechanical preparation and commercial judgement.

Mechanical preparation includes collecting data, aligning dates, matching fields, calculating totals, checking completeness and producing the same views every week. That part should be automated where the data sources are stable enough.

Commercial judgement includes deciding what the numbers mean. Why did margin drop? Which supplier is causing stock-outs? Which marketplace is growing but not profitable? Which slow-moving stock needs action? That work belongs with people.

The test: if the same person rebuilds the same report every week before they can start thinking, the process is doing work in the wrong order.

The same principle applies to other repetitive workflows. The article on checking the process before hiring another ecommerce administrator explains why good people should spend more time on judgement and less time carrying data between systems.

Define the decisions before the metrics

Do not start by listing every number the business could see. Start with the decisions the team must make on Monday.

  • Do we need to reorder stock this week?
  • Has gross margin fallen below an agreed threshold?
  • Is one sales channel growing at the expense of profit?
  • Which products need pricing, listing or stock action?
  • Which supplier or operational issue needs an owner today?

Once the decisions are clear, the metrics become easier to choose. This prevents the common mistake of building a large dashboard with no agreed action behind it.

I would rather see six trusted measures with clear owners than sixty measures that invite debate about definitions. Reporting quality usually improves when the business removes numbers, not when it adds them.

What the report should show

Sales and gross margin

Show revenue, gross margin and material movements against the prior period or plan. Revenue without margin can reward the wrong behaviour.

Stock cover and availability

Flag fast-moving products close to running out, slow-moving stock tying up cash and supplier deliveries that need attention.

Channel performance

Shopify, Amazon, eBay and wholesale may tell different stories. Compare them on consistent definitions rather than trusting each platform's headline numbers.

Data and operational exceptions

Show failed feeds, missing product records, large price changes and orders that did not follow the expected process.

Actions and owners

Every material exception should have a named owner, a due date and an allowed next step.

Where stock numbers disagree across channels, the problem may sit upstream rather than in the report. The guide to why stock synchronisation fails between Shopify, Amazon and eBay covers the ownership and timing issues that often create false availability.

Build one controlled data route

A reliable report should have a defined source for every figure. That may be Shopify for orders, the accounting system for recognised revenue and fees, the warehouse or ERP for stock, and a product master for SKU ownership.

Each source should feed a standard reporting layer. The workflow then checks expected files, reporting dates, duplicate records, missing fields and material changes before producing the final output.

This is similar to deciding whether to integrate systems or automate the hand-off between them. A full integration may be justified for stable, high-volume flows. A controlled export-and-validation process may be safer for smaller or less stable operations.

The UK Government's data quality framework highlights dimensions such as accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness and validity. Those ideas translate well into weekly reporting: every report should define what “good enough to use” means before it is distributed.

Use exceptions, not endless tables

A strong Monday report should show what changed, what failed and what needs a decision. The team should not have to scan hundreds of rows to discover that three products, one supplier and one marketplace account explain the week's risk.

Set thresholds deliberately. A margin movement of 0.2 percentage points may be noise. A fall of 4 points in a major category may need an owner. A stock discrepancy of two units may be acceptable. A negative available quantity should not be.

My prediction is that weekly reporting will become shorter as automation improves. Machines are good at producing detail. The more valuable skill will be designing the rules that decide which detail deserves human attention.

Do not automate an untrusted report

If nobody agrees on what revenue, gross margin, stock or returns mean, automating the report will reproduce the argument faster.

For Shopify finance reporting, read the guide to reconciling Shopify payouts, orders and fees. A payout is not the same as sales revenue, and platform deductions can distort a simplistic weekly view.

Before building anything, document definitions, source ownership, cut-off times and known adjustments. Then compare the automated output with several prior reports before relying on it.

The Office for National Statistics' guidance on quality in statistics stresses that quality should be considered in relation to user needs. The commercial version is straightforward: a precise figure that does not support a decision is still a poor reporting choice.

Measure the payback properly

If a weekly report takes four hours to build, that is about 208 hours a year before corrections and follow-up questions. At a loaded staff cost of £30 an hour, preparation alone costs roughly £6,240 a year.

If automation reduces preparation to 45 minutes of review, it releases about 169 hours of annual capacity, worth roughly £5,070 at the same rate. That is not automatically cash saved, but it is capacity the business can redirect into buying, supplier management, product work or customer issues.

The stronger value may be earlier action. One prevented stock-out, caught margin issue or corrected feed failure can matter more than the time saving. The automation ROI framework explains how to separate labour capacity, avoided risk and measurable commercial impact.

My rule is to approve reporting automation only when the business can name both the workload removed and the decisions improved. Saving time without improving action is useful, but it is not the full value.

A practical four-step build

  1. Collect: gather the last four reports, source files and manual adjustments.
  2. Define: agree each metric, source, cut-off, threshold, owner and decision.
  3. Test: run the automated version beside the manual report and investigate every difference.
  4. Operate: schedule the report before Monday, retain logs and review exceptions rather than rebuilding the pack.

I think the best future reporting systems will look less like dashboards and more like operating briefs. They will explain what changed, why it matters and who needs to act, while preserving a route back to the underlying data.

FAQs

What should an ecommerce Monday report include?

It should usually include sales, gross margin, stock cover, channel performance, cash-impacting exceptions and a short list of decisions that need attention.

Should weekly reports be fully automated?

Data collection, calculations, validation and distribution can often be automated. Commercial interpretation and action should remain with the operator.

How do you know whether a weekly report is useful?

A useful report has named owners, clear thresholds and decisions attached to each section. If nobody acts on it, simplify or remove it.

What is the main risk of manual reporting?

Manual reporting wastes time, creates version and formula risk, and delays decisions that should be visible at the start of the week.

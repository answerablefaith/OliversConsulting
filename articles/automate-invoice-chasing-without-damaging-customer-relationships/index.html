How to automate invoice chasing without damaging customer relationships

Automate routine reminders, overdue flags and follow-up tasks. Keep disputes, commercial judgement and sensitive conversations with a person.

Published 21 August 2026Updated 21 August 2026By Henry OliverFinance and back-office automation
Quick answer

Invoice chasing is a good automation candidate when the workflow can tell the difference between a normal overdue invoice and an exception. Automate reminders, status checks and task creation. Stop the sequence when payment, a dispute, a payment plan or a human-owned conversation changes the situation.

Late-payment admin often looks simple from the outside. Send a reminder, wait, send another, then call. In practice, finance teams spend time checking whether an invoice was paid, whether the customer queried it, whether someone already promised a revised date, and whether the account should be handled differently.

That is why invoice chasing should be automated as a state-based workflow, not as a calendar that fires increasingly stern emails.

My view: the best credit-control automation makes routine follow-up more consistent while making exceptions more visible. If it makes every customer receive the same treatment, it is probably too crude.

What parts of invoice chasing should be automated?

Start with work that follows clear rules. Check whether an invoice has reached its due date. Confirm whether payment has been recorded. Send a reminder when the account is still open. Create a follow-up task when a response needs review. Stop the sequence when the status changes.

These steps are repetitive and easy to define. They are different from deciding whether to extend terms, accept a payment plan, waive a charge, escalate a dispute or change how an important customer is handled.

This fits the broader pattern behind automating invoice processing without losing approval control: automate movement and checks first, then keep judgement at the point where money, risk or customer treatment changes.

Use invoice states instead of one overdue flag

A single overdue field is not enough. It tells you a date has passed, but it does not tell you what should happen next. A more useful workflow gives each invoice a small number of operational states.

StateWhat it meansAutomation action
Due soonThe due date is approaching and no issue is recordedOptionally send a polite pre-due reminder or do nothing, depending on policy
Overdue, no responseThe invoice is unpaid and there is no recorded dispute or arrangementSend the next reminder and set the next review point
Customer respondedThe customer replied but the account still needs actionPause generic reminders and create a task for the right person
Disputed or query openThe customer has challenged the invoice or needs informationStop chasing and route the issue to the owner of the query
Payment arrangementA different payment date or plan has been agreedUse the agreed schedule rather than the standard overdue sequence
PaidPayment has been recorded against the invoiceStop all reminders and close outstanding tasks

The key control is that a customer response changes the workflow. An automation that keeps sending “your invoice is overdue” after the customer has raised a valid query is not saving admin. It is creating another problem for someone to repair.

What should an automated reminder actually contain?

Keep the message factual. Identify the invoice, amount, due date and a clear route to reply. If your process can provide a payment link or statement safely, include it. Avoid invented urgency or language that assumes the customer is refusing to pay when the system only knows that payment has not been matched.

The sequence should also change with the state. A first reminder can be a simple prompt. A later message can note that the invoice remains outstanding and ask whether there is a query. Once a customer has replied, the workflow should stop pretending that no conversation exists.

If your finance team already spends time reconciling records before chasing, fix that dependency too. The guide to automating supplier statement reconciliation without hiding exceptions shows the same design principle from the payables side: reliable status comes before automated action.

When should the automation stop and hand over?

Build explicit stop rules before building the reminder sequence. At minimum, stop when payment is recorded, a dispute is raised, the customer proposes a payment arrangement, an internal owner takes over, or the account enters a special handling process.

You may also want different handling for strategic customers, unusually large balances or accounts where another commercial issue is already open. These rules should come from your real operating policy rather than from the automation tool.

This is where many workflows fail. They are designed around the happy path, then exceptions are added later. I would reverse that. Define the conditions under which the automation must stop before deciding how many reminders it should send.

How much admin can this remove?

Measure your current work rather than relying on generic savings claims. For example, suppose a business has 140 open invoices each month and 35 require at least one manual reminder. If checking status, finding the right contact, writing the email and recording the follow-up takes six minutes per invoice, that is 210 minutes of routine work before any second reminder or phone call.

An automated workflow does not remove all of that. It can remove the repeated status checks and first-line follow-up for clean cases, leaving the team to spend its time on the invoices that genuinely need judgement.

That distinction matters. Automation value usually comes from reducing repeated low-value touches, not from pretending the whole credit-control function can run without people.

What data does the workflow need?

At a minimum, it needs a reliable invoice identifier, customer contact, due date, outstanding balance, payment status and a place to record disputes or special arrangements. It also needs a clear owner for exceptions.

If those fields live in several systems, avoid creating another spreadsheet just for the automation unless it has a defined role. How should a small business start?

  • Map the current reminder sequence and who owns each exception.
  • Define the invoice states that actually change what happens next.
  • Write stop rules before reminder rules.
  • Start with one customer group or one invoice type.
  • Check that payment status is reliable before sending anything automatically.
  • Review the exceptions after the first few cycles and refine the rules.

What should stay human?

Keep disputes, negotiation, payment plans, complaints, changes to commercial terms and sensitive customer conversations with a person. The automation should bring those cases forward with the right context. It should not make the decision on their behalf.

That boundary is also good customer experience. Routine customers get fast, consistent reminders. Customers with a genuine issue do not have to fight a sequence that cannot understand them.

Common questions

Can invoice chasing be automated?

Yes. Routine reminders, overdue checks and internal follow-up tasks are strong candidates. Exceptions and commercial decisions should stay with a person.

When should automated invoice reminders stop?

Stop when payment is recorded, a dispute is raised, a different arrangement is agreed or a person takes ownership of the conversation.

How often should reminders be sent?

Use intervals that fit your payment terms, customer relationships and credit-control policy. More reminders are not automatically better.

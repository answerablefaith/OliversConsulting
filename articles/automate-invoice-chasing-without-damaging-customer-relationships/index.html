How to automate invoice chasing without damaging customer relationships

Automate routine reminders, overdue checks and follow-up tasks. Keep disputes, commercial judgement and sensitive conversations with a person.

Published 21 August 2026Updated 21 August 2026By Henry OliverFinance and back-office automation
Quick answer

Invoice chasing can be automated safely when the workflow knows more than the due date. It needs reliable payment status, a small set of invoice states and clear stop rules. Automate routine reminders and internal follow-up; hand disputes, payment arrangements and sensitive customer conversations to a person as soon as the situation stops being routine.

Late-payment admin looks simple until you watch someone do it properly. Before sending a reminder, they may check the bank, accounting system, inbox, CRM notes and previous promises. The email itself takes seconds. Establishing whether it should be sent can take much longer.

That is the useful automation opportunity. The goal is not to make the chasing more aggressive. It is to remove repeated checking while making exceptions easier to see.

My view: good credit-control automation should reduce awkward conversations, not create more of them. If a system keeps chasing a customer who has already raised a query, the problem is not the wording of the reminder. The workflow has failed to understand its own state.

What parts of invoice chasing should you automate?

Start with decisions that can be written as rules. Has the due date passed? Is the invoice still outstanding? Has the customer already replied? Is there an open dispute or agreed payment date? Who owns the next action?

From there, an automation can check status, send an appropriate reminder, record the contact, create a task and schedule the next review. It can also stop itself when the facts change.

Keep decisions that alter commercial treatment with people: extending terms, accepting a payment plan, waiving charges, escalating a complaint or deciding how to handle an important customer. The same principle applies when automating invoice processing without losing approval control: automate movement and repeatable checks before judgement.

Use invoice states instead of one overdue flag

An overdue flag only tells you that a date has passed. It does not tell the workflow what the business now knows. A small state model is much more useful.

StateWhat it meansWhat the automation should do
Due soonNo issue is recorded and the due date is approachingSend a pre-due prompt only if that suits your policy
Overdue, no responseThe invoice is unpaid and no exception is recordedSend the next reminder and set the next review point
Customer respondedA reply needs attentionPause generic reminders and create a task
DisputedThe customer has queried the invoice or supporting workStop chasing and route the issue to the right owner
Payment arrangementA different date or plan has been agreedFollow the agreed schedule, not the standard sequence
PaidPayment has been recordedClose reminders and outstanding follow-up tasks

The most important transition is often not “overdue” to “paid”. It is “overdue” to “someone has told us something”. That response should change the process immediately.

What should an automated invoice reminder contain?

Keep it factual and easy to act on. Include the invoice number, amount, due date and a clear route to reply. If your process can safely provide a statement or payment link, include it. Do not manufacture urgency when the system only knows that a payment has not yet been matched.

A first reminder can be short. A later one can ask whether there is a query. Once the customer replies, stop sending messages that behave as though the conversation never happened.

The Office of the Small Business Commissioner recommends checking the payment terms and invoice details, then using a polite reminder and direct contact where necessary. Its guidance on dealing with unpaid invoices is a useful reference for UK small businesses.

Build stop rules before reminder rules

Many automations are designed around the happy path first: day 1 email, day 7 email, day 14 email. Exceptions are bolted on later. I would reverse that.

Define the situations in which the automation must stop before deciding how often it should chase. Payment recorded, dispute opened, payment plan agreed, complaint raised, account put on hold and human ownership are obvious examples. Your own policy may add others.

This changes the character of the system. It becomes an exception-routing workflow with reminders attached, rather than an email sequence with safety checks added afterwards.

I think that distinction will matter more as AI improves. Generating a persuasive reminder is already easy. The harder and more valuable problem is deciding whether a reminder should be sent at all, what context it should carry and when a human should take over.

What data and systems need to connect?

At minimum, the workflow needs a reliable invoice identifier, customer contact, due date, outstanding balance, payment status and somewhere to record disputes or special arrangements. It also needs a named owner for exceptions.

That data may sit across accounting software, bank feeds, email and a CRM. Do not create another spreadsheet merely to bridge them unless it has a clear purpose and owner. If the hand-off between systems is the real problem, the guide on whether to integrate systems or automate the hand-off is a better starting point.

If the team is manually reconciling records before it can chase confidently, fix that dependency too. Reliable status should come before automated action, which is the same control principle used in supplier statement reconciliation.

How should a UK SME start small?

  • Take one month of overdue invoices and note every reason a normal reminder was not appropriate.
  • Turn those reasons into a small set of states and stop rules.
  • Choose one customer group or invoice type for the pilot.
  • Automate the first routine reminder and the internal follow-up task, not the whole credit-control process.
  • Check false reminders and missed exceptions after each cycle.
  • Only add later stages once staff trust the status data.

There is also a useful upstream lesson here. The Small Business Commissioner advises businesses to get invoice details, payment terms and the correct recipient right before chasing becomes necessary. Its guide to getting invoices right is worth using as part of the process review.

Worked example

Suppose 35 invoices need a first reminder each month and the manual check, email and record update takes six minutes each. That is 210 minutes of routine work before second reminders or calls. If the workflow safely handles 25 of those cases and routes 10 exceptions to people, the benefit is not “credit control is automated”. It is that the team spends less time proving the obvious and more time resolving the cases that actually need attention.

What should stay human?

Keep disputes, negotiation, payment plans, complaints, changes to commercial terms and sensitive customer conversations with a person. Automation should bring those cases forward with the right invoice history and context.

There is a second reason for that boundary: a late invoice can be a finance problem, but it can also reveal a delivery problem, a relationship problem or a process error. A person can connect those signals. A reminder sequence usually cannot.

My preference is therefore quite conservative: automate the routine majority only when the workflow can recognise the non-routine minority. If you cannot reliably tell those two groups apart, improve the process data before adding more automation.

Common questions

Can invoice chasing be automated?

Yes. Routine reminders, overdue checks, follow-up timing and internal task creation are strong candidates. Disputes, payment plans, complaints and commercial decisions should stay with a person.

When should automated invoice reminders stop?

Stop when payment is recorded, a dispute is raised, a different arrangement is agreed, an account is placed on hold or a person takes ownership of the conversation.

How often should reminders be sent?

There is no universal interval. Use a sequence that fits your payment terms, customer relationships and credit-control policy. More reminders are not automatically better.

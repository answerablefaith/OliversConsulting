How to automate expense claims without losing approval control

Automate capture, checks and routing for routine claims. Keep policy exceptions, unusual spend and reimbursement judgement with the right person.

Published 28 August 2026Updated 28 August 2026By Henry OliverFinance and back-office automation
Quick answer

Expense claims are a strong automation candidate when the workflow can reliably collect the claimant, date, amount, category, receipt and cost centre, then route the claim according to clear approval rules. Automate the repetitive checks and hand-offs. Stop claims that are incomplete, outside policy or otherwise unusual before reimbursement.

Expense administration is rarely difficult because adding up receipts is hard. It is difficult because claims arrive in different formats, receipts go missing, managers approve by email, finance teams re-key the same information and policy exceptions are mixed in with ordinary spend.

That makes expense claims a good candidate for workflow automation, but only if the process separates routine claims from judgement-heavy exceptions.

My view: the useful automation is not the one that approves everything faster. It is the one that makes normal claims almost frictionless and unusual claims impossible to overlook.

What parts of expense claims should be automated?

Start with deterministic work. Capture the claimant and claim date. Require the amount, category, receipt and business purpose. Check whether mandatory fields are present. Route the claim to the correct manager or cost owner. Record the approval decision. Send approved claims to the next finance step and return rejected or incomplete claims to the claimant with a clear reason.

This is the same design principle behind automating invoice processing without losing approval control: move clean records quickly, but keep the exceptions visible.

Use claim states instead of one submitted flag

StateWhat it meansWorkflow action
DraftThe employee has started a claim but required information is missingKeep it with the claimant and show the missing fields
SubmittedRequired data is present and the claim is ready for checksValidate receipt, category and routing fields
ExceptionThe claim breaches a defined rule or needs judgementStop automatic progression and route to the appropriate reviewer
Awaiting approvalThe claim passed basic checks and needs an authorised decisionCreate the approval task and record the owner
ApprovedThe authorised approver accepted the claimSend it to the next finance or reimbursement step
ReturnedThe claim needs correction or more evidenceSend it back with the reason and preserve the history

The important part is that the workflow knows why a claim cannot continue. A missing receipt is different from a policy exception, and both are different from a manager simply not having reviewed the claim yet.

What information should be captured once?

At minimum, capture the employee, date, supplier or merchant, amount, currency where relevant, category, business purpose, receipt and the team, project or cost centre that should carry the spend. The exact fields will depend on the business, but each field should have a clear purpose.

If employees currently send receipts through email, chat and shared folders, fix the intake route first. The comparison of email attachments, shared folders and structured forms explains why consistent input usually matters more than adding another approval tool.

What should stay in human review?

Keep decisions that depend on context with a person. That includes unusual business purpose, disputed categories, exceptional spend, missing evidence that needs explanation, senior-level exceptions, duplicate-looking claims that are not obviously duplicates, and anything that changes the firm's normal treatment of the expense.

The workflow can highlight the relevant facts and show which rule was triggered. It should not quietly convert an exception into an approval simply because every required field is filled in.

How should approval routing work?

Approval should follow the operating rules of the business, not the convenience of the automation platform. A simple workflow may route by team or manager. A more mature one may consider claim value, category, project, cost centre or exception type.

A useful rule is to avoid unnecessary approvals. If every low-value routine claim goes through three people, automation may only make a poor process faster. Use approval steps where they add control, and remove duplicate checks where they do not.

What should happen after approval?

Do not make finance staff re-key an already approved claim if the next system can receive the required fields safely. The workflow should move the approved record, preserve the original evidence and keep a link back to the approval history.

Where systems do not connect cleanly, you do not automatically need a full integration. The guide on whether to integrate systems or automate the hand-off can help decide whether a controlled transfer is enough.

How do you stop duplicate or incomplete claims?

Use deterministic checks where possible. A missing receipt, blank business purpose or absent cost centre can be stopped immediately. Potential duplicates can be flagged using combinations such as claimant, date, merchant and amount, but a person should review uncertain matches before rejecting a legitimate claim.

The same principle applies across data-heavy workflows: automation is strongest when clear checks happen before the record reaches the next stage. Our article on catching product-data errors before listings go live uses the same upstream-control logic in a different operating context.

How do you know whether expense automation is worth it?

Measure the repetitive work rather than assuming every claim needs automation. Count how many claims arrive each month, how often finance re-keys information, how many claims are returned for missing data, how long managers spend finding context before approval, and how many different channels employees use to submit evidence.

The strongest candidates usually have a high volume of routine claims with stable policy rules and a smaller set of exceptions that genuinely need attention.

What should a first version include?

  • One standard claim form or capture route.
  • A small set of mandatory fields.
  • Clear states for incomplete, standard and exceptional claims.
  • One defined approval route for ordinary claims.
  • Explicit stop rules for missing evidence and policy exceptions.
  • A named owner for every exception type.
  • A reliable hand-off into the next finance step.

Start with one employee group or one expense category if the wider process is messy. Prove the routing and exception logic before expanding it.

Where should a UK SME start?

Map one claim from employee submission to reimbursement. Mark every place the same information is copied, every approval, every policy check and every reason finance sends the claim back. Then separate the checks that can be described as rules from the decisions that require judgement.

That gives you a practical automation boundary: routine capture and movement on one side, human judgement on the other.

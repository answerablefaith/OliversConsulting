How to automate expense claims without losing approval control

Automate capture, checks and routing for routine claims. Keep policy exceptions, unusual spend and reimbursement judgement with the right person.

Published 28 August 2026Updated 28 August 2026By Henry OliverFinance and back-office automation
Quick answer

UK SMEs can automate expense claims by capturing the same required fields once, checking them against clear rules and routing complete claims to the right approver. Missing receipts, possible duplicates, unusual spend and policy exceptions should stop before reimbursement so automation removes admin without hiding the decisions that matter.

Expense administration is rarely difficult because adding up receipts is hard. It is difficult because claims arrive in different formats, receipts go missing, managers approve by email, finance teams re-key the same information and policy exceptions are mixed in with ordinary spend.

The useful automation target is the repeated handling around the decision, not the decision itself.

My view: the strongest expense workflow makes an ordinary claim boring. The employee submits it once, the system checks the obvious things, and finance only sees the cases where something actually needs judgement.

What parts of expense claims should you automate?

Start with work that can be described as rules. Capture the claimant, date, merchant, amount, category, receipt and business purpose. Check that required fields are present. Look up the employee's manager or cost owner from a controlled table. Route the claim. Record the decision. Move approved data into the next finance step.

Name the mechanism at least once. Receipt capture might use a form plus OCR to suggest the merchant, date and total. A validation rule can then compare those extracted values with what the claimant entered. OCR is useful for reducing typing, but the submitted claim should still be the record that follows the approval process.

This follows the same control principle as automating invoice processing without losing approval control: move clean records quickly and make the exceptions harder to miss.

Use claim states instead of one submitted flag

A single submitted status tells finance almost nothing. A small state model shows what the claim needs next.

StateWhat it meansWorkflow action
DraftRequired information is still missingKeep the claim with the employee and show the gaps
SubmittedRequired fields are presentRun completeness, duplicate and policy checks
ExceptionA rule failed or the claim needs judgementStop automatic progression and show the reason
Awaiting approvalBasic checks passed and an authorised decision is neededCreate the approval task and record the owner
ApprovedThe authorised approver accepted the claimSend it to the next finance or reimbursement step
ReturnedThe claim needs correction or more evidenceSend it back with the reason and keep the history

The distinction matters. A missing receipt is not the same problem as a manager who has not replied, and neither is the same as a claim outside policy. When every problem appears as “pending”, finance still has to investigate each one manually.

I would design the exception states before the approval route. Approvals are easy to automate. The harder part is deciding what should never reach an approver as a normal claim in the first place.

What information should employees enter only once?

For most businesses, the useful core is claimant, date, merchant, amount, currency where relevant, category, business purpose, receipt and the team, project or cost centre that should carry the spend. Add fields only when somebody uses them for a real decision, posting or control.

If employees currently send evidence through email, chat and shared folders, fix the intake route first. The comparison of email attachments, shared folders and structured forms explains why consistent input often removes more admin than adding another approval screen.

A less obvious benefit is better rework. When a claim is returned, the employee should see exactly what is missing. “Expense rejected” creates another conversation. “Receipt missing” or “business purpose required” lets the employee fix it without asking finance what went wrong.

How should duplicate and policy checks work?

Use deterministic checks first. A missing receipt, blank business purpose or absent cost centre can stop immediately. A possible duplicate can be flagged by comparing combinations such as claimant, date, merchant and amount. The workflow can score or flag the match, but uncertain cases should stay with a person.

Policy checks should work the same way. Encode rules the business has actually agreed, such as categories that always need review or value bands that need a different approver. Do not let the automation invent a policy because the current process is inconsistent.

I would rather see a conservative duplicate check create a few extra reviews than have an aggressive rule automatically reject legitimate claims. False positives are visible work. A wrong rejection can damage trust in the whole process.

What should stay in human review?

Keep decisions that depend on context with a person. That includes unusual business purpose, exceptional spend, missing evidence that needs explanation, possible duplicates that are not obvious, and anything that changes the firm's normal treatment of the expense.

The workflow should gather the evidence around the decision and show which rule triggered the review. It should not quietly turn an exception into an approval because every box is filled in.

As AI improves, I expect more of the preparation to disappear from finance teams. Models can already help classify receipts or summarise a claimant's explanation. The valuable boundary is still the same: use AI to prepare the case faster, not to make an unexplained judgement about whether unusual spend is acceptable.

How should approval routing work?

Approval should follow the operating rules of the business, not the layout of the automation platform. A simple route may use team and manager. A more mature one may also use claim value, category, project, cost centre or exception type.

Avoid blanket approval chains. If every £15 train fare goes through three people, automation only makes a poor process move faster. Keep approval where it adds control and remove duplicate checks where it does not.

When the next finance system cannot receive the record directly, a controlled hand-off may be enough. The guide on whether to integrate systems or automate the hand-off can help keep the first build proportionate.

How do you know whether expense automation is worth it?

Measure the repeated work before buying or building anything. Count monthly claims, how often finance re-keys data, how many are returned for missing information, how long managers spend finding context, and how many submission channels employees use.

The strongest candidates have a high volume of ordinary claims, stable rules and a smaller set of genuine exceptions. If the policy changes every week or nobody agrees who should approve what, the first job is process design rather than automation.

My preference is to judge the workflow by how little investigation a clean claim needs and how quickly an exception explains itself. A high automation percentage means very little if finance still opens every claim to make sure the system was right.

What should a first version include?

  • One standard claim form or capture route.
  • A small set of mandatory fields.
  • Clear states for incomplete, standard and exceptional claims.
  • One defined approval route for ordinary claims.
  • Explicit stop rules for missing evidence, possible duplicates and policy exceptions.
  • A named owner for every exception type.
  • A reliable hand-off into the next finance step.

Start with one employee group or one expense category if the wider process is messy. Prove the capture, routing and exception logic before expanding it.

Where should a UK SME start?

Map one claim from employee submission to reimbursement. Mark every place the same information is copied, every approval, every policy check and every reason finance sends the claim back. Then split the process into rules and judgement.

That usually exposes the first useful build: one controlled intake route, a few clear checks, one approval path and a visible exception queue. You do not need to rebuild the whole finance stack to remove a large amount of repeated admin.

Common questions

Can expense claims be automated?

Yes. Routine capture, completeness checks, approval routing and finance hand-offs can be automated when the rules are clear. Missing evidence, unusual spend and policy exceptions should stay in review.

What should stop an automated expense claim?

Typical stop conditions include a missing receipt, incomplete business purpose, suspected duplicate, policy exception, unusual amount or a claim that needs a different approver.

What data is needed for expense claim automation?

A useful workflow needs the claimant, date, merchant, amount, category, business purpose, receipt, cost centre or project and enough routing data to identify the correct approver.

Fix the operating rules before replacing your spreadsheet

A new system will not fix a process when nobody owns the data, agrees the definitions or knows who can make the final decision.

Published 29 July 2026Updated 19 August 2026By Henry OliverUK SME operations

Quick answer

Keep the spreadsheet if it has one purpose, clear owners, agreed definitions and controlled change rights. Replace or wrap it only when the process needs concurrent editing, role-based approvals, reliable audit history or frequent system-to-system data movement. Software should implement agreed rules, not substitute for them.

Key takeaways:

  • Define purpose, ownership, definitions and change rights before changing tools.
  • Test the rules by asking two experienced users to process the same awkward case.
  • Keep a governed spreadsheet when volume and risk are modest.
  • Move beyond it when workflow, approval, audit or integration needs exceed the file.

Spreadsheets take the blame for many operational failures. The file is called fragile, manual or out of control. Sometimes that is fair. More often, the spreadsheet is exposing a management problem that existed before the file was opened.

I have worked with large data controls and run a multi-channel ecommerce operation. The same pattern appears at both ends of the scale. A tool becomes unreliable when the business has not agreed what the data means, who owns it, which changes are allowed and what happens when two people disagree.

My view: do not replace a spreadsheet until you can explain the operating rules it is meant to enforce. Otherwise, you will move the same confusion into a more expensive system.

A spreadsheet can hold data. It cannot settle responsibility.

Consider a weekly order tracker. Sales updates promised dates. Operations updates dispatch dates. Finance adds payment status. Customer service changes priorities. By Friday, four teams have touched the same rows, but no one can say which field is authoritative.

The common response is to buy a workflow platform or ask for an integration. That may improve access and history. It will not answer whether sales can override operations, whether an unpaid order can be released, or who owns a missing customer reference.

This is why system projects often disappoint. The software is asked to resolve a decision the business has avoided making.

I think this is one of the most expensive misunderstandings in small-business technology. A company believes it is buying clarity, but it is really buying a more polished place for old disagreements to continue.

The four operating contracts

Before automating a spreadsheet-heavy process, I would expect four short agreements. They do not need to be legal documents. They need to be clear enough that two people would handle the same row in the same way.

Contract 1

Purpose

State the decision the file supports. A stock sheet for purchasing is not the same as a stock sheet for customer promises. One file should not quietly serve five conflicting purposes.

Contract 2

Ownership

Name the owner of each critical field and the owner of the process. Shared access is not shared accountability. Someone must decide which value wins.

Contract 3

Definitions

Define terms such as active customer, available stock, overdue invoice or completed order. A green cell is useless when each team applies a different rule.

Contract 4

Change rights

Decide who may edit, approve, correct or override. Record the reason for material changes. Automation needs these rights before it can route work safely.

These four contracts also give you a practical test for whether the process is ready for automation. Ask two experienced users to process the same awkward example independently. If they reach different outcomes, the gap is not technical. The operating rule is still missing.

Most spreadsheet errors begin before the formula

Formula errors are real, but they are visible and testable. The harder problems are vague inputs and silent choices. A user types “urgent” with no agreed meaning. A date is changed because a supplier made a promise on the phone. A blank value means “not known” to one person and “not required” to another.

Current government data-quality guidance makes a useful point: data rules should define what good data looks like, and their importance should reflect the impact of failure. The principle applies well beyond government. A business should not treat a missing delivery note with the same weight as a missing comment.

Good automation starts by separating critical rules from useful preferences. It checks the critical fields, shows the exceptions and leaves a record of the decision. This is the same control principle used when catching product-data errors before listings go live: risky records stop, while valid work continues.

My preference is to use fewer rules, but make them meaningful. A long checklist that nobody trusts is weaker than six controls that directly protect cash, customers or operational capacity.

When should the spreadsheet stay?

Keep it when the process is low volume, the users are known, the rules are stable and the file has a clear owner. A well-designed spreadsheet can be a sensible operational tool. It can also be the fastest way to test a process before building anything deeper.

Government guidance on creating and sharing spreadsheets recommends clear versioning, consistent column names and documented code. Those are not cosmetic details. They reduce the chance that two copies, two labels or one hidden macro become competing versions of the truth.

A useful spreadsheet should also make its controls visible. Protect formula cells. Separate inputs from calculations. Use controlled lists for important statuses. Show the last refresh time. Keep an owner and review date on the first sheet. These are small changes, but they reduce uncertainty without forcing the business into a larger system before the case exists.

Do not automate ambiguity. If the team cannot agree who owns a field or what a status means, pause the build. Run the process manually with the new rules first. The disagreement is useful evidence.

When has the spreadsheet reached its limit?

Move beyond it when several people edit at once, the process needs role-based approvals, changes need a reliable audit trail, data must move between systems, or errors create material cost. The case becomes stronger when the file depends on macros that only one person understands.

Other warning signs include repeated duplicate copies, manual consolidation across teams, delays caused by one absent employee, and frequent repair after imports or exports. Those are signs that the file is now carrying workflow, approval and integration responsibilities it was never designed to manage.

At that point, the right answer may be a controlled workflow, a database, a direct integration or a better front end around the existing systems. The choice should follow the process. My prediction is that the best SME automation projects will increasingly start with governance rather than software selection. The firms that define ownership, evidence and exception rules first will build smaller systems, make fewer corrections and change providers more easily later.

Build the business case before replacing the file

Do not justify a replacement with statements such as “the spreadsheet is messy”. Measure the operational cost. Count hours spent combining versions, correcting entries, checking formulas, chasing missing fields and rebuilding reports. Add the cost of delays, repeated customer contact or decisions made from stale data where you can evidence it.

Then define the target. For example: reduce six weekly hours of consolidation to one hour of exception review, eliminate duplicate version handling and give every critical change an owner. That is a business outcome. “Move the spreadsheet into a platform” is only a technical action.

Use conservative assumptions and include licence, build, support and change-management costs. Treat any worked figures as operational estimates, not guaranteed savings or individual financial advice. The automation ROI framework gives a simple way to test payback without treating every saved minute as cash.

Fix the operating model before buying the replacement

Start with one live process. Write the purpose in one sentence. Mark the critical fields. Name each field owner. Define the statuses. List the allowed changes and the evidence needed for an override.

Then watch one complete cycle. Where do people disagree? Which fields arrive late? Which decision waits for one person? That is the real specification for improvement.

Once the rules are settled, automation becomes simpler. Data can be checked at entry, changes can follow approval rights, and exceptions can reach the correct owner. You are no longer automating a spreadsheet. You are automating an agreed operating process.

That distinction matters. A bad spreadsheet is often a symptom. Replacing the symptom without fixing the rules gives the business a cleaner screen and the same old uncertainty.

I would rather improve one spreadsheet-backed process until the rules are trusted than rush into a broad replacement that users work around. Trust is the real migration plan. Without it, the old file usually survives beside the new system.

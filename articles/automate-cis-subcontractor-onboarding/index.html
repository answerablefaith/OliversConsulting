How to automate CIS subcontractor onboarding without losing control

The useful automation collects the facts once and keeps the HMRC decision visible. It does not guess who someone is or what deduction rate should apply.

Published 19 August 2026Updated 19 August 2026By Henry OliverConstruction operations
Quick answer

Automate CIS subcontractor onboarding by collecting the required identity details once, checking completeness and prior records, routing the person through the correct HMRC verification step, then storing the verification result and deduction treatment against one subcontractor record. Stop the workflow when identity, employment status or verification evidence is unclear.

Construction firms often have the same subcontractor details scattered across email, spreadsheets, accounting software and site records. The admin feels small each time: ask for a UTR, copy a company number, check whether someone has been used before, record the HMRC result, then re-enter the same details when payment is prepared.

The risk is not only wasted time. CIS onboarding contains decisions that should be traceable. If the workflow hides where a deduction rate came from, or treats a new subcontractor as verified because a spreadsheet says so, automation has made the control weaker.

My view: automate the preparation, checking and record movement around CIS. Keep the official verification result and the employment-status decision as explicit control points.

What does HMRC actually require before payment?

HMRC says a contractor needs to verify a new subcontractor before paying them. The verification tells the contractor whether the subcontractor is registered for CIS and what deduction rate to use, or whether they can be paid without a deduction.

The details used for verification depend on the type of subcontractor. A sole trader may require a UTR and National Insurance number. A limited company needs its company name, company UTR and registration number. Partnerships require their own set of details. HMRC also says the details supplied must exactly match the information used to register.

That is a good automation boundary. A workflow can check whether the required fields are present and correctly formatted. It should not invent a missing identifier or choose a deduction rate itself. The rate should come from the HMRC CIS verification process or supported commercial software.

There is another important boundary before CIS verification. A contractor's monthly return includes a declaration about subcontractors not being employees. HMRC warns that giving the wrong employment status can lead to a penalty. That judgement should not be reduced to a hidden automation rule.

Use a six-gate CIS onboarding control map

I would design the workflow as six gates. Each gate either passes with evidence or stops with a reason.

This is different from a generic onboarding form. The point is not simply to get every box filled. It is to preserve why the subcontractor is ready for payment and where each decision came from.

Do you need to verify the same subcontractor again?

Not always. HMRC says a subcontractor normally does not need to be verified again if you included them on a CIS return in the current tax year or either of the previous two tax years.

That rule is useful for automation because it is deterministic. Your workflow can look for a matching subcontractor record and the date of the last qualifying CIS return. If the evidence is present, the record can bypass unnecessary re-verification. If the history is missing or ambiguous, send it to review.

The key is to check your own evidence, not rely on someone's memory. “We used them last year” is not a control. A dated record linked to the relevant subcontractor is.

What should one subcontractor record contain?

Keep the master record small enough to trust. It should contain the identity fields needed for the business and CIS process, the status decision owner, verification date, verification reference or evidence, returned deduction treatment, payment details needed by finance and a clear last-used date.

Do not turn the onboarding record into a dumping ground for every document exchanged with the subcontractor. Link to source documents where they are needed. Keep each field because a downstream process or control needs it.

This is the same principle behind creating one trusted client record: collect a fact once, decide who owns it and route only the fields each destination requires.

How should CIS records flow into finance?

Once the verification result is recorded, the next hand-off is normally payment preparation. The workflow can pass the subcontractor identity, approved deduction treatment and payment reference into the accounting or payment process.

It should also keep the payment evidence needed later. HMRC says contractors must keep records of the gross amount invoiced by subcontractors, deductions made and, where deductions apply, relevant materials costs. These records must be kept for at least three years after the end of the tax year they relate to.

A controlled workflow can therefore create a CIS-ready payment record as the transaction is processed, rather than asking finance to rebuild the evidence at month end. The official CIS record-keeping guidance is a useful checklist for the fields your process needs to preserve.

What should never be automated away?

Do not let the workflow decide that a person is genuinely self-employed because they supplied a UTR. Do not infer a deduction rate from an old invoice. Do not replace a failed verification with a default because a payment is urgent.

Those are not data-movement problems. They are control decisions.

The same applies to monthly returns. HMRC requires contractors to report payments made to subcontractors each month. A workflow can prepare the reporting dataset, reconcile it to payments and show missing fields. The authorised filing step should remain visible, with the source records available for review. See HMRC's monthly return guidance for the current filing requirements.

What does the time saving look like?

Measure the admin around onboarding, not the HMRC decision itself. Count the time spent requesting missing details, checking previous records, creating supplier records, copying verification outcomes and preparing payment fields.

Worked example

Suppose a contractor onboards 18 subcontractors a month. The current process takes 16 minutes per person across emails, spreadsheets and finance setup, or about 4.8 hours a month. If a controlled workflow reduces the routine preparation and record creation to 5 minutes of review per person, that falls to 1.5 hours. At £30 an hour, the released capacity is about £99 a month before software and support costs. The stronger case is often cleaner evidence and fewer payment delays rather than labour alone.

Use your own figures. If the firm uses two subcontractors a year, a clear checklist may be enough. Automation earns its place when the process repeats, several people touch the record or month-end reporting depends on information being reconstructed.

How should a construction firm pilot the workflow?

Take the last ten subcontractors added to the business. Rebuild the path from first request through verification and first payment. Mark every point where a detail was retyped, chased, corrected or copied into another system.

Then define the six gates. Decide who owns the status decision, which identity fields are mandatory, how you prove recent CIS history, how the HMRC result is captured and what finance needs before payment.

Run the new workflow in parallel for a short period. Track missing fields, duplicate warnings, verification stops and payment mismatches. A false pass is more serious than a visible stop, so tune the rules to be conservative.

If subcontractor details currently arrive through mixed messages and attachments, the earlier article on choosing the right intake channel can help standardise the source. If finance is still re-keying approved data between systems, use the integration versus hand-off framework to decide how far to connect the process.

The useful end state

A subcontractor should not have one identity in an email, another in a spreadsheet and a third in the accounting system. The business should have one controlled record showing who the subcontractor is, who confirmed the status route, when verification was performed, what HMRC returned and what evidence supports the payment treatment.

That is what makes CIS onboarding automation useful. It removes repeated admin without hiding the points where judgement and official verification still matter.

CIS subcontractor onboarding FAQs

Can CIS subcontractor onboarding be automated?

Yes. You can automate data collection, completeness checks, duplicate checks, record creation, reminders and storage of verification outcomes. Keep employment-status decisions and official verification controlled and evidenced.

Do you need to verify every subcontractor every time?

No. HMRC says you normally do not need to verify a subcontractor again if you included them on a CIS return in the current tax year or either of the previous two tax years.

How long should CIS records be kept?

HMRC says relevant CIS payment, deduction and materials records must be kept for at least three years after the end of the tax year they relate to.

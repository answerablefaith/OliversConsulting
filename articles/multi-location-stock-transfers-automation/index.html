Multi-location stock transfers: what should be automatic and what should stay in review?

Moving stock between locations is a good automation candidate, but only after you define what counts as a safe transfer.

Published 14 August 2026By Henry OliverEcommerce & distribution

When one warehouse has too much stock and another is running low, the obvious answer is to move units. The difficult part is deciding when software should recommend that move, when it can prepare the transfer, and when a person should stop it.

My view: automate the repeatable allocation maths first. Keep unusual demand, stock discrepancies and commercially important exceptions visible to a person.

The problem is not just seeing stock

Multi-location businesses can have enough stock overall and still disappoint customers. Ten units in a slow location do not help much when the location fulfilling today's orders has none.

Current Shopify guidance puts location-level visibility, formal receiving and channel-aware allocation at the centre of multi-location inventory management. UK government guidance also warns that selling across several online channels makes manual inventory control more complex and raises the risk of overselling.

The practical issue is therefore allocation. Which location needs stock, which location can release it, and what evidence should block the move?

Use four transfer states

A useful automation does not treat every imbalance as permission to move stock. I would separate transfer decisions into four states.

StateTypical conditionAutomation action
RecommendDestination is below its agreed threshold and another location has genuine surplusCalculate source, destination and suggested quantity
PrepareSKU identity, available stock and transfer rules all passCreate a draft transfer for approval or warehouse action
ReviewDemand has changed sharply, stock is reserved, or the transfer would consume a safety bufferShow the reason and require a person to decide
StopNegative stock, unresolved count difference, missing SKU mapping or another data-control failureCreate an exception; do not move stock

This is more useful than a rule such as “move stock when location A drops below five”. A threshold without context can simply move the shortage somewhere else.

Define available stock before you automate

The number shown as “on hand” is not always the number you can safely transfer. Some units may already be committed to customer orders, wholesale allocations, bundles or another transfer.

Define an available-to-transfer quantity. A simple version is:

physical or trusted system stock − customer commitments − protected channel allocation − safety buffer − open outbound transfers.

Your systems may calculate this differently. The important point is that everyone agrees which number the workflow is allowed to use.

This is similar to the control problem behind stock sync across Shopify, Amazon and eBay: ownership and timing rules matter as much as the integration itself.

Do not let yesterday's sales make today's transfer decision alone

Recent sales are useful, but demand is not flat. Promotions, weekends, wholesale orders, weather and product launches can change what a location needs.

A transfer rule can use a small set of understandable inputs: current available stock, recent sales rate, minimum cover, open orders and stock already in transit. If one input moves outside a normal range, send the recommendation to review rather than pretending the forecast is certain.

Shopify's 2026 inventory guidance recommends defined replenishment thresholds and notes that transfer and receiving errors often occur when stock moves between locations. That supports a controlled approach: automate the repetitive calculation, but keep discrepancy handling explicit.

Receiving is part of the transfer

A transfer is not finished when stock leaves the source. The destination has to confirm what arrived.

If 40 units were shipped and 38 arrived, the workflow should not silently close the transfer as 40. Record the received quantity, flag the two-unit difference and keep the inventory adjustment traceable.

This is where simple automation creates more value than another spreadsheet. The same record can carry the transfer from recommendation to draft, dispatch, receipt and exception.

Start with recommendations, not full autonomy

For a first version, I would run the rules daily and produce a short transfer queue. Each row should show the SKU, source, destination, proposed quantity and reason.

Then measure what happens for two to four weeks:

  • How many recommendations were accepted unchanged?
  • Which rules caused people to override the quantity?
  • How often was the system stock wrong?
  • Which locations or products generated most exceptions?
  • Did a transfer create a shortage at the source?

If a class of recommendations is consistently accepted and the underlying stock data is reliable, that class can move from recommendation to automatic draft creation. Do not automate the exceptions merely to increase the automation percentage.

When should you use a dedicated inventory system?

If you have many locations, complex bundles, manufacturing, frequent partial receipts or sophisticated forecasting needs, a proper inventory or warehouse platform may be the better answer. The current Shopify ecosystem includes tools for multi-location inventory, replenishment and transfer workflows, so a custom build should solve a real gap rather than duplicate a mature product.

If your problem is smaller, a controlled workflow between existing systems may be enough. Use the integration versus automated hand-off framework before buying or building a large system.

The control test

Before automating a stock transfer, ask one question: could the workflow explain why it wants to move this quantity today?

If the answer is yes, and the inputs are reliable, the repeated calculation is a strong automation candidate. If the answer is no, adding more automation will only move uncertain stock faster.

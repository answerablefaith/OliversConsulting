Automation priority guide

What should a small business automate first?

Start with work that repeats often, follows clear rules, uses usable data and creates a visible cost or delay. The best first automation is usually boring, measurable and easy to verify.

Published 11 September 2026Updated 11 September 2026By Henry OliverAutomation strategy and decision content
Quick answer

A UK small business should usually automate a process that is frequent, reasonably rules-based, supported by usable data, measurable in time or delay, and low enough in risk to test safely. Re-keying information, routine status updates, standard document handling, recurring reports and straightforward routing are often better first candidates than judgement-heavy customer or commercial decisions.

The first automation matters because it teaches the business how automation behaves with real data, real exceptions and real ownership.

The wrong first project is often too ambitious. It crosses several systems, tries to solve inconsistent data and depends on decisions nobody has written down. The right first project is narrower. It proves that one recurring piece of work can move faster without making the process harder to understand.

My view: do not start with the process that sounds most impressive. Start with the process where success and failure are easiest to recognise.

Use the five-factor automation priority test

Score each candidate process against five questions. The framework is deliberately operational rather than technical.

FactorStrong candidateWeak candidate
FrequencyHappens daily or weeklyOccurs occasionally or unpredictably
Rule clarityPeople can explain the normal route and stop rulesDifferent people make the same decision differently
Data readinessInputs are accessible and reasonably consistentCritical fields are missing, duplicated or hard to identify
RiskErrors are visible and recoverableA wrong action could create material customer, financial or compliance impact
ValueThe process consumes measurable time, creates delay or causes repeated reworkThe benefit is vague or mainly theoretical

A strong first process does not need a perfect score. But if frequency, rule clarity and data readiness are all weak, automation is unlikely to fix the underlying problem.

Simple scoring method

Score each factor from 1 to 5. A process scoring 20 or more out of 25 is worth investigating first, provided no single risk factor makes automation inappropriate. The number is a prioritisation aid, not a universal benchmark.

Why frequency usually matters first

Frequent work creates repeated opportunity. A ten-minute task performed twice a year is rarely a priority. A five-minute task performed twenty times a day can consume meaningful capacity, especially when it interrupts other work.

Measure the whole process rather than only the typing time. Setup, finding files, checking inputs and restarting interrupted work all matter. Our guide to measuring the true cost of manual admin shows how to build that baseline without pretending every minute becomes cash saving.

What types of work are usually good first automation candidates?

The strongest candidates tend to move, check, assemble or route information that already exists.

  • Repeated data entry: moving customer, order or finance fields between systems.
  • Routine notifications: acknowledgements, reminders or internal alerts triggered by a known event.
  • Document handling: collecting files, checking required information and routing complete items to the right owner.
  • Recurring reports: collecting the same data, applying the same checks and producing the same structure.
  • Reconciliation: matching records and surfacing exceptions for review.
  • Status routing: assigning standard work based on clear categories or conditions.

The cross-system data-entry guide is a good example of a first automation pattern: the normal transfer can run automatically, while ambiguous matches stop for review.

What should not be your first automation?

Do not start with a process simply because it is annoying.

Delay automation when the team cannot agree on the correct output, the source data cannot be trusted, exceptions dominate the workload, or success depends mainly on nuanced judgement.

A broken process can be automated, but it usually becomes a faster broken process. The article on fixing operating rules before replacing a spreadsheet explains why ownership and definitions matter before tooling.

I would also avoid making the first project a large two-way integration unless the business genuinely needs one. A controlled hand-off may prove the operating rules with less complexity. The integration-versus-hand-off decision guide explains that trade-off.

Should your first automation use AI?

Only if AI solves a real input problem.

If the workflow uses structured fields and exact rules, conventional automation is often easier to test. AI becomes useful when the inputs vary: free-text emails, inconsistent descriptions, documents or classification work where fixed rules become brittle.

Even then, keep the stable parts deterministic where possible. AI can interpret or suggest; explicit rules can validate, route and stop high-impact actions.

The goal is not to prove that the business can use AI. It is to improve a process.

How do you compare two good automation candidates?

When two processes both score well, prefer the one with the clearer baseline and shorter feedback loop.

For example, suppose one team manually copies approved customer details into a finance system, while another team manually compiles a monthly board pack from several spreadsheets. Both may be automatable. The customer-data hand-off may be the better first project because it happens daily, has a small number of fields and can be checked record by record.

The board pack may still be valuable, but its success depends on more sources, more definitions and more judgement around commentary.

How much should the first automation cost?

Do not choose a project because it is cheap. Choose a project where the business case is explainable and the scope is controlled.

Cost depends on the workflow, systems, data quality, exceptions, controls and support needs. Our guide to business automation cost for UK SMEs explains those drivers and the current Olivers Consulting service bands.

A smaller first build has another advantage: it gives the business evidence about how much exception handling, maintenance and internal review are actually required before a larger programme begins.

What should you prepare before building?

Bring six things:

  • the process trigger;
  • the normal steps;
  • a few real examples of the inputs;
  • the exceptions that stop the normal route;
  • the person who owns the outcome;
  • one measurable sign that the new process is better.

That last point matters. “We automated it” is not a success measure. Better measures might be reduced handling time, fewer duplicate records, faster routing, a smaller backlog or fewer corrections.

A practical first-automation shortlist

If you are unsure where to begin, list every recurring admin process your team complains about. Remove anything that happens rarely. Remove anything whose correct outcome nobody can explain. Remove anything where the data cannot currently be trusted.

Score what remains using the five factors above. Take the top two or three and compare the expected value with the build effort and risk.

That gives you a shortlist based on how the business actually works rather than on whichever automation tool was demonstrated most recently.

Common questions

What should a small business automate first?

Start with frequent, rules-based work that uses reasonably structured data, creates measurable time or delay and is low enough risk to test safely.

What business processes are best for automation?

Repeated data movement, routine notifications, recurring reports, standard document handling, reconciliations and clear routing workflows are common strong candidates.

What should not be automated first?

Avoid beginning with processes whose rules are disputed, data is unreliable, exceptions dominate or the outcome depends mainly on nuanced judgement. Fix the process first or automate only the stable preparation around it.

Automation priority guide

What should a small business automate first?

Start with work that repeats often, follows clear rules, uses usable data and creates a visible cost or delay. The best first automation is usually boring, measurable and easy to verify.

Published 11 September 2026Updated 11 September 2026By Henry OliverAutomation strategy and decision content
Quick answer

A UK small business should usually automate a frequent, rules-based process with usable data, a measurable cost or delay, and errors that are easy to spot and recover from. Re-keying data, recurring reports, routine document checks and standard routing are often better first projects than judgement-heavy customer, pricing or approval decisions.

The first automation matters because it teaches the business how automation behaves with real data, real exceptions and real ownership.

The wrong first project is often too ambitious. It crosses several systems, tries to repair inconsistent data and depends on decisions nobody has written down. The right first project is narrower. It proves that one recurring piece of work can move faster without making the process harder to understand.

My view: do not start with the process that sounds most impressive. Start with the process where success and failure are easiest to recognise.

How do you choose what to automate first?

Use five questions: how often the work happens, how clear the rules are, whether the data is usable, how risky a wrong action would be and whether the benefit can be measured.

FactorStrong candidateWeak candidate
FrequencyHappens daily or weeklyOccurs occasionally or unpredictably
Rule clarityPeople can explain the normal route and stop rulesDifferent people make the same decision differently
Data readinessInputs are accessible and reasonably consistentCritical fields are missing, duplicated or hard to identify
RiskErrors are visible and recoverableA wrong action could create material customer, financial or compliance impact
ValueThe process consumes measurable time, creates delay or causes repeated reworkThe benefit is vague or mainly theoretical

A process does not need a perfect score. But if frequency, rule clarity and data readiness are all weak, automation is unlikely to fix the underlying problem.

Simple scoring method

Score each factor from 1 to 5. A process scoring 20 or more out of 25 is worth investigating first, provided no single risk factor makes automation inappropriate. The number is a prioritisation aid, not an industry benchmark.

I would use the score to narrow the shortlist, not to outsource the decision. A 22-point process with sensitive approvals may still be a worse first project than an 18-point data hand-off that is easy to test and reverse.

Why does frequency matter so much?

Frequent work creates repeated opportunity. A ten-minute task performed twice a year is rarely a priority. A five-minute task performed twenty times a day can consume meaningful capacity, especially when it interrupts other work.

Measure more than typing time. Include opening systems, finding the right record, checking the input, correcting errors and restarting after interruptions. Our guide to measuring the true cost of manual admin shows how to build that baseline without pretending every saved minute becomes cash.

Frequency also gives you faster feedback. A daily process produces enough real cases to expose weak rules quickly. That makes it a better learning project than something that runs once a quarter.

What business processes are usually good first automation candidates?

The strongest candidates tend to move, check, assemble or route information that already exists.

  • Repeated data entry: moving customer, order or finance fields between systems using stable identifiers or lookup rules.
  • Routine notifications: sending an acknowledgement or internal alert when a known event occurs.
  • Document handling: checking required fields or file presence against a template before routing the case onward.
  • Recurring reports: importing the same source files, applying the same calculations and flagging failed checks.
  • Reconciliation: matching records by reference, date or amount within an agreed tolerance and surfacing the unmatched items.
  • Status routing: assigning standard work based on clear categories, thresholds or ownership rules.

The cross-system data-entry guide is a good example. A workflow can use a customer ID or order reference to find the right destination record, move agreed fields and stop when more than one match is possible.

What should a small business avoid automating first?

Do not start with a process simply because it is annoying.

Delay automation when the team cannot agree on the correct output, the source data cannot be trusted, exceptions dominate the workload, or success depends mainly on nuanced judgement.

A broken process can be automated, but it usually becomes a faster broken process. The article on fixing operating rules before replacing a spreadsheet explains why ownership and definitions matter before tooling.

I would be especially cautious when the first project can send money, change customer entitlements, approve unusual pricing or make a decision that is hard to reverse. Automate the preparation around those decisions first. Leave the approval itself with a person until the rules and evidence are proven.

I would also avoid making the first project a large two-way integration unless the business genuinely needs one. A controlled hand-off may prove the operating rules with less complexity. The integration-versus-hand-off decision guide explains that trade-off.

Should the first automation use AI?

Only if AI solves a real input problem.

If the workflow uses structured fields and exact rules, conventional automation is often easier to test. AI becomes useful when inputs vary, such as free-text emails, inconsistent descriptions or documents where a fixed parsing rule is too brittle.

Even then, keep the stable parts deterministic where possible. A model might classify an email or extract a likely reference. A lookup rule can then validate the reference, and a stop rule can send uncertain cases to review.

My expectation is that AI will make messy inputs easier to prepare, but it will not remove the need to define ownership, controls and exceptions. The expensive mistakes usually happen after interpretation, when a workflow takes the wrong action with confidence.

How do you compare two good automation candidates?

When two processes both score well, prefer the one with the clearer baseline, shorter feedback loop and easier rollback.

Suppose one team manually copies approved customer details into a finance system, while another team manually compiles a monthly board pack from several spreadsheets. Both may be automatable. The customer-data hand-off may be the better first project because it happens daily, has a small number of fields and can be checked record by record.

The board pack may still be valuable, but its success depends on more sources, more definitions and more judgement around commentary.

My preference is to choose the process that teaches you something useful even if the first version is modest. A small workflow can expose data problems, ownership gaps and exception patterns before you commit to a larger programme.

How much should the first automation cost?

Do not choose a project because it is cheap. Choose a project where the business case is explainable and the scope is controlled.

A smaller first build has another advantage: it gives the business evidence about how much exception handling, maintenance and internal review are actually required before a larger programme begins.

What should you prepare before building?

Bring six things:

  • the event that starts the process;
  • the normal steps;
  • a few real examples of the inputs;
  • the exceptions that stop the normal route;
  • the person who owns the outcome;
  • one measurable sign that the new process is better.

That last point matters. “We automated it” is not a success measure. Pick something observable: fewer duplicate records, less re-keying, shorter turnaround, fewer missing fields, less report preparation or a smaller exception backlog.

I would also write down how the workflow can fail. If nobody can explain what happens when a record is missing, a system is unavailable or a rule does not apply, the process is not ready to run unattended.

What is a sensible first automation for a UK SME?

A sensible first project is narrow enough to test but important enough to matter. One trigger, one clear outcome and a visible exception route is often enough.

Examples include moving approved customer data from a CRM into finance software, assembling a weekly operational report from known sources, checking whether required onboarding documents are present, or matching routine records and sending only the exceptions to a person.

The point is not to maximise the percentage of work automated on day one. It is to create one workflow the team trusts. Once people stop checking every result manually, you have a stronger base for the next project.

Common questions

What should a small business automate first?

Start with a frequent, rules-based process that uses reasonably structured data, has a measurable cost or delay and is low enough risk to test safely.

What business processes are best for automation?

Good candidates repeat often, follow stable rules, use accessible data and have outputs that are easy to verify. Re-keying, routine notifications, reconciliations, report assembly and standard routing are common examples.

What should not be automated first?

Avoid starting with work whose rules are disputed, data is unreliable, exceptions dominate, or the output depends mainly on nuanced judgement. Fix the process first or automate only the stable preparation around it.

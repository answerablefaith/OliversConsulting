Hiring versus automation

Before you hire another ecommerce admin, check the process first

A growing ecommerce team may genuinely need another person. But when the proposed role is dominated by repeated data movement, adding headcount can hide a process problem rather than solve it.

Published 7 July 2026Updated 26 July 2026By Henry OliverUK ecommerce & wholesale
Quick answer

Before hiring another ecommerce administrator, map the work you expect them to absorb. Separate commercial judgement from repeatable data movement, measure the hours and error risk, and test whether clearer rules or controlled automation can remove the recurring workload. Then hire for ownership and decisions, not for copying information between systems.

Hiring can be the right answer. Strong operators, merchandisers and customer-facing staff create real value. The problem starts when a capable person is recruited mainly to clean spreadsheets, rebuild upload files, copy orders, chase reports or keep several systems aligned.

I saw this at scale before starting Olivers Consulting. I ran a multi-channel ecommerce operation with around 200,000 SKUs across Shopify, Amazon and eBay. At that volume, small administrative leaks become permanent operating costs. A supplier file, stock correction or Monday report is not merely a task. It is a workflow with inputs, rules, exceptions and an output.

My view is that headcount should increase the judgement capacity of a business. When a new hire is immediately buried in repeated data handling, the company has bought relief without fixing the cause.

What are you actually hiring the person to do?

Read the proposed job description line by line. Phrases such as “maintain spreadsheets”, “update listings”, “compile weekly reports”, “process supplier files”, “enter orders into Sage” and “keep stock aligned” are warning signs.

They do not prove the role should be automated. They show where investigation should begin. Some product updates need merchandising judgement. Some wholesale orders contain customer-specific exceptions. The useful question is which steps follow stable rules and which require experience.

If supplier-file preparation is a large part of the role, review the process for automating supplier price lists safely. If the job exists because staff repeatedly type orders into finance software, examine how to stop re-keying wholesale orders into Sage or Xero.

Separate judgement from data movement

Divide the proposed role into two columns:

  • Judgement: choosing ranges, negotiating with suppliers, resolving customer issues, interpreting unusual results, deciding what to promote and approving commercial exceptions.
  • Movement: downloading files, renaming columns, applying fixed rules, matching records, copying values, preparing uploads, creating standard reports and routing failed rows.

Judgement belongs with people. Predictable movement is where automation should be assessed. Most useful systems do not eliminate the employee. They reduce the mechanical layer so the employee can handle decisions and exceptions.

The Home Office engineering principle on automating to eliminate manual steps notes that manual processes increase cost, introduce error and slow teams down. That principle applies well beyond software teams: repetitive operational steps create the same friction in ecommerce.

A practical test: if a trained colleague can follow the task from a written rule set with little judgement, it is a candidate for automation. If the correct answer changes with customer context, supplier behaviour or commercial priorities, retain human ownership.

Map the pressure before choosing the solution

Choose a normal four-week period and log the work you think requires another administrator. Record the trigger, source, systems touched, handling time, rework and person responsible.

Also count interruptions. A process that takes 40 minutes may consume much more capacity if it breaks concentration six times a week. Note where work waits for a file, approval or correction. That delay may be a hand-off problem rather than a staffing shortage.

For each workflow, write the desired output and the stop conditions. For example: “Create the weekly channel report by 10am, but stop if order totals differ from finance by more than £250.” This turns a vague capacity complaint into a process that can be redesigned.

The same approach supports automating recurring ecommerce reports and reducing the key-person dependency hidden inside admin work.

Compare the full cost, not salary against software

A weak comparison uses annual salary versus build price. A better comparison includes loaded employment cost, recruitment effort, onboarding, management time, software subscriptions, maintenance, risk and released capacity.

Illustrative capacity calculation

Suppose four recurring workflows consume 18 hours a week. At a loaded staff cost of £29 an hour, that is £522 a week, or £27,144 across 52 weeks. If process changes and automation reduce the workload to five review hours, 13 hours of weekly capacity are released. That is about £19,604 of annual capacity, before valuing fewer errors or faster turnaround.

Released capacity is not automatically cash saved. It becomes valuable when the team uses it for work that would otherwise require overtime, delayed projects or another hire. Use the automation ROI framework to make the assumptions visible.

I prefer conservative business cases. If a proposal only works when every minute becomes cash, every exception disappears and maintenance costs nothing, it is not a credible proposal.

When should you still hire?

Hire when the business needs ownership, judgement and communication. A growing catalogue may need someone to improve range structure, challenge supplier quality, coordinate launches and make commercial decisions. Automation cannot take responsibility for an unclear operating model.

Hiring may also be the right first step when the process changes daily or nobody can explain the rules. A capable operator can stabilise the work, document the decisions and create the conditions for later automation.

Recruitment itself should be deliberate. Acas provides practical guidance on how employers should recruit, including defining the role, advertising and selecting candidates. A process review strengthens that work because it removes low-value tasks from the role before the job description is published.

My prediction is that the strongest ecommerce teams will not become “people-light” in a simplistic sense. They will become judgement-heavy. Routine movement will increasingly happen in controlled workflows, while smaller teams own more valuable decisions.

A five-step decision before approving headcount

  1. List the recurring tasks: use actual examples from the last month, not broad job-title language.
  2. Measure the workload: include handling, checking, waiting, correcting and interruptions.
  3. Separate rules from judgement: identify which decisions must remain with a named person.
  4. Price three options: improve the process, automate the stable steps, and hire for the remaining role.
  5. Run a small test: redesign one high-volume workflow before committing to a permanent solution.

Do not assume the result must be automation. The answer might be a clearer spreadsheet, a better hand-off or a new employee with a more valuable role. The guide to choosing between integration and controlled automation can help define the lightest safe solution.

I think businesses will increasingly treat process design as part of workforce planning. That is a healthier direction than treating automation and hiring as opposing choices. Good process design tells you what technology should handle and what people should own.

FAQs

When should an ecommerce business automate instead of hiring?

Check automation first when the workload is recurring, rule-based and dominated by data movement, such as supplier files, stock updates, reporting or order re-keying.

When is hiring still the better option?

Hiring is usually better when the role needs judgement, supplier negotiation, merchandising, customer communication, accountability or ownership of a changing process.

How do you compare the cost of hiring with automation?

Measure the hours, loaded employment cost, recruitment and onboarding effort, error risk, automation build cost, maintenance and the value of released capacity.

Should automation remove people from the process?

Not necessarily. Strong workflows automate predictable movement while keeping people responsible for approvals, exceptions and commercial decisions.

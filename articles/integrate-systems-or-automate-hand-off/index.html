Commercial decision guide

Should you integrate your systems or automate the hand-off?

A full integration is not always the best answer. Sometimes the right move is to control the data hand-off around the systems you already have.

Published 24 July 2026By Henry OliverUK SME operations

When two systems do not talk to each other, the first instinct is often to build an integration. That can be right. It can also be expensive, slow and unnecessary.

The better question is not, “Can these systems connect?” It is, “What must move between them, how often, under which rules, and what happens when the data is wrong?”

That distinction matters for UK SMEs. A full API integration may be ideal for a stable, high-volume flow. A controlled automation may be better when files, emails or exports already contain the right information. A clearer manual process may still win when volume is low or judgement dominates.

My rule: integrate stable systems when the flow is frequent and predictable. Automate the hand-off when the systems can stay as they are but the repeated data movement should not remain manual.

The integration decision matrix

How often does the task run?Many times each dayDaily, weekly or in batchesOccasionally
How stable are the inputs?Stable APIs and fieldsMostly stable files, emails or exportsVariable and judgement-heavy
How costly is a failed transfer?High, with automated monitoringModerate, with review and stop rulesLow, with clear ownership
How much transformation is needed?Limited and consistentMapping, cleaning and validationCase-by-case interpretation
What is the likely best fit?Direct system-to-system connectionWorkflow around existing toolsShort checklist and named owner

When does a full integration make sense?

A full integration is strongest when the process runs often, the systems have reliable APIs and the data rules are settled. Examples include frequent order updates, stock movements or customer records that must stay aligned throughout the day.

The build still needs controls. A connection that moves bad data faster is not an improvement. Define the source of truth, field mapping, retry rules, duplicate handling, alerts and an audit trail before anything goes live.

This is the same lesson behind unreliable stock sync between Shopify, Amazon and eBay. The technical connection is only one part. Ownership and business rules decide whether the result is safe.

When should you automate the hand-off instead?

Many SME processes do not need a permanent live connection. They need a repeatable way to collect, check, transform and move data between existing systems.

For example, a team may export invoices from one platform, add customer references, check totals and upload the result into finance software. A controlled automation can collect the export, map the fields, flag missing references and produce a review-ready file. The finance system does not need to change.

This approach is often faster to prove because it follows the real operating rhythm. It also keeps review visible. Bad rows can stop instead of entering the next system silently.

The principle is similar to stopping wholesale orders from being re-keyed into Sage or Xero: remove the repeated typing while keeping the approval and exception decisions with the team.

When is manual still the right answer?

Do not automate a task simply because it is annoying. If it happens twice a month, takes ten minutes and depends on commercial judgement, a clear checklist may be the better investment.

Manual work becomes a problem when it is frequent, rules-based, error-prone or difficult to recover. Low-volume judgement work is different. The cost of building and maintaining automation can exceed the value released.

This is why ROI must be based on the real process, not on a broad promise. The automation ROI framework gives a practical way to test hours, loaded cost, error risk and payback before committing.

Use this four-step test before choosing

  1. Map the hand-off. Name the source, destination, fields, frequency, owner and current failure points.
  2. Separate transfer from judgement. Identify which parts move data and which parts need a person to decide.
  3. Choose the lightest safe option. Start with a checklist, controlled automation or full integration based on the matrix above.
  4. Define proof. Measure cycle time, rework, failed transfers, manual touches and recovery effort before and after.

Suppose a team spends five hours each week exporting, cleaning and re-entering data between two systems. At a loaded cost of £28 an hour, that is £140 a week. If a controlled automation removes four hours and costs £2,400 to build, the simple capacity payback is about 21 working weeks. That does not include avoided errors, so treat those as additional evidence rather than invented savings.

At that point, the choice becomes commercial. A full integration may still be right if the flow will grow or needs near-live updates. A controlled hand-off may be better if the systems, file formats or business rules continue to change.

What should you do next?

Pick one system hand-off that causes repeated copying, checking or reconciliation. Write down what enters, what leaves and every decision in between.

Then test whether the process needs a permanent connection or a controlled workflow around the tools you already use. The goal is not the most technical solution. It is the smallest reliable solution that removes repeated work without hiding risk.

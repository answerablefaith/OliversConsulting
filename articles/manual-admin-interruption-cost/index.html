Manual admin costs more than the minutes you count

The expensive part of a ten-minute admin task is often what it interrupts.

Published 12 August 2026By Henry OliverUK SME operations

When businesses price repetitive admin, they usually count the task itself. Ten minutes to update a tracker. Five minutes to move data into the CRM. Fifteen minutes to rebuild a report. That maths misses the bigger cost: the interruption around the task.

My view: if a repeated task keeps pulling a skilled person out of useful work, measure the interruption before you decide the task is too small to automate.

A ten-minute task can take more than ten minutes from the day

Imagine an operations manager is reviewing a supplier problem. They have the context in their head: the order history, the margin impact and the decision they need to make. Then an email arrives asking them to copy figures into a weekly tracker.

The update takes ten minutes. But the manager also has to stop the original work, find the right file, check the figures, make the update and then rebuild the context they had before. The clock on the admin task only records one part of the loss.

This matters because repetitive work rarely arrives in one neat block. It arrives between calls, during analysis, before a customer reply and while someone is trying to finish a decision.

Measure four costs, not one

I use a simple interruption-cost view when a task looks too small to justify attention. It separates the visible admin from the work around it.

CostWhat to measureExample
Task timeMinutes spent doing the repeated stepCopying sales figures into a tracker
Setup timeTime spent finding files, emails or system recordsOpening three exports and checking the right date
Restart timeTime needed to return to the interrupted workRe-reading notes before continuing an analysis
Delay costUseful work that moves later because admin took priorityA quote, customer decision or exception review waits

You do not need academic precision. Measure a normal week. The aim is to expose whether a five-minute task is really five minutes, or whether it creates a much larger break in the day.

The frequency matters more than the size

A two-hour task once a year may not deserve automation. A seven-minute task that happens twelve times a day might.

Frequency changes the economics because setup and restart costs repeat too. It also increases the chance that people rush, skip checks or postpone the task until a pile has formed.

Worked example

Suppose a manager handles eight small admin interruptions a day. Each task takes six minutes, with two minutes to find the right information and three minutes to get back into the previous work. That is 11 minutes of disruption each time, or 88 minutes a day. Across 220 working days, that is about 323 hours a year. At an employment cost of £35 an hour, the capacity involved is about £11,300 a year.

That does not mean an automation is automatically worth £11,300. Some interruptions still need judgement. Some restart time would happen anyway. Use the number as a signal to investigate, not as a sales claim.

Do not automate the judgement just to remove the interruption

The right design often removes preparation and data movement while leaving the decision with the person.

A sales lead may still decide the next action after a call. The workflow can capture the agreed fields and prepare the CRM update. A finance manager may still decide what to do with an unmatched supplier balance. The workflow can perform the routine matching first. A manager may still approve a report. The numbers can arrive already collected and checked.

This is the same reason I favour controlled workflows over vague promises of autonomy. The goal is not to remove people. It is to stop using their attention for work that follows a known rule.

Batching is useful, but it is not always the answer

One response to interruptions is to batch the admin. That works when the task can wait. Process all updates at 4pm. Review one exception queue twice a day. Build the report once rather than every time someone asks for a number.

But batching has limits. If customers are waiting, stock decisions depend on the update or a finance control has a deadline, the delay may create another problem. In those cases, automation can move the routine step when the event happens and send only the decision to a person.

If work is arriving through several inboxes and file types, first choose a better intake route for the process. If the problem is movement between systems, use the integration versus automated hand-off framework before building anything large.

Run an interruption audit for five working days

Do not ask staff to list every annoying task from memory. For one week, record interruptions as they happen.

  • What triggered the task?
  • Who had to stop what they were doing?
  • How long did the task itself take?
  • How much time went into finding inputs or checking them?
  • Did the person have to re-enter data already held elsewhere?
  • Was judgement required, or only for the exceptions?
  • Could the task have waited and been batched?

At the end of the week, rank tasks by annual frequency and total disruption. Then look at the top three. That gives you a better automation shortlist than starting with whichever software demo looked impressive.

What should you automate first?

Start where the trigger is clear, the inputs are available, the rule is stable and failure can be detected. Good candidates often include report preparation, CRM updates, document routing, reconciliation, data checks and repeated system hand-offs.

Avoid automating a messy decision simply because it interrupts people. Fix ownership and rules first. Our article on missing operating rules explains why a new tool cannot repair an undefined process.

The best first project is often boring. It quietly removes dozens of small interruptions without changing who owns the important decision. That is exactly why it works.
